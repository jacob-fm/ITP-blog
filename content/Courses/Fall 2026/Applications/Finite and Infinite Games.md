I'm coming into this with a small sliver of prior context. In undergrad, I read a bit C. Thi Nguyen's _Games: Agency as Art_ (ok, literally just the introduction) and listened to a few interviews of his for an independent study. So I have some familiarity with the talk about classifying games.

In the intro to _Games: Agency as Art_, I recall that Nguyen borrows definitions from other philosophers and explains that he is only going to be describing a certain type of games in his book, where (among other things) there is a goal that is either met or not met, rather than things like playing "house" as a child, where there is no goal to be met. I'm curious to see how Carse's distinctions relate.

## Chapter 1: There Are at Least Two Kinds of Games

- _"It is an invariable principle of all play, finite and infinite, that whoever plays, plays freely. Whoever must play, cannot play."_
  - I've had this thought before about NBA teams that tank, or individual players that are pulling shenanigans trying to get out of contracts
- All this talk about wars and electricians has me wondering if the definition of game we're coming to is much broader than I expected
- _"Only one person or team can win a finite game, but the other contestants may well be ranked at the conclusion of play."_
  - seems ripe for argumentation
- _"There are no rules that require us to obey rules. If there were, there would have to be a rule for those rules, and so on."_
  - AKA there is no objective reason to do anything
  - this is exactly the line of thinking began my skepticism of the Orthodox Judaism I was raised in when I was in high school
- _"It may appear that the prizes for winning are indispensable, that without them life is meaningless, perhaps even impossible. There are, to be sure, games in which the stakes seem to be life and death. In slavery, for example, or severe political oppression, the refusal to play the demanded role may be paid for with terrible suffering or death."_
  - a broad definition of game indeed
- _"To account for the large gap between the actual freedom of finite players to step off the field of play at any time and the experienced necessity to stay at the struggle, we can say that as finite players we somehow veil this freedom from ourselves."_
  - Nguyen talks about the difference between _goal_ and _purpose_ in games - the goal may be to get more points than the other team, while the purpose may be to win money/glory/fame/bragging rights
- seems like the role of "mother" is considered a finite game by Carse, which is confusing
  - it's only end is death, which of course ends all games
  - I'm curious when we will get an example of an infinite game

> _We cannot say a person played this infinite game or that, as though the rules are independent of the concrete circumstances of play. It can be said only that these persons played with each other and in such a way that what they began cannot be finished._

- sounds like an infinite game describes a dynamic or relationship between people, or between a person and the world/environment around them
- _"Dramatically, one chooses to be a mother; theatrically, one takes on the role of mother."_
  - I am not clear on this
- _"Surprise is a crucial element in most finite games. If we are not prepared to meet each of the possible moves of an opponent, our chances of losing are most certainly increased."_
  - although, some moments of utmost drama take place when there is little surprise, only a contest of will
  - e.g. a player defending the basket sees a player start an attempt to dunk and goes to block it
  - both players know what the other is going to do, so the variety of outcomes is a function only of who does it better
- _"There is a contradiction here: If the prize for winning finite play is life, then the players are not properly alive."_
  - seems to me there's an unstated premise here - that a prize must be something which the player doesn't yet possess, that it cannot be the maintenance of something they already have
- _"The contradiction of finite play is that the players desire to bring play to an end for themselves."_
  - this is one of a number of assertions that have me scratching my head
  - if we are considering death as the end of a finite game for many, than surely most of those finite players do not desire the end of that game
- _"Infinite players play best when they become least necessary to the continuation of play."_
  - earlier he says that playfulness is all about acknowledging that there _are_ consequences to our actions??
- _"If one has sufficient power to win before the game has begun, what follows is not a game at all"_
  - would be curious to read some examples of things that **not** games

> _Evil arises in the honored belief that history can be tidied up, brought to a sensible conclusion. I t is evil to act as though the past is bringing us to a specifiable end. It is evil to assume that the past will make sense only if we bring it to an issue we have clearly in view. It is evil for a nation to believe it is "the last, best hope on earth." It is evil to think history is to end with a return to Zion, or with the classless society, or with the Islamicization of all living infidels._

- it must then be evil to have an ideology at all
  - all ideologies have end goals which are believed will "put things right"
  - this applies to any political or religious ideology, at least all the Western ones

### Reflections on Chapter 1

Really enjoying this so far. I've been going pretty slow and for the most part, I can say I understand the larger flow of the arguments being made, though many details escape me. I'm skeptical about a number of assertions made, some of which seem to be arbitrary or contradictory to earlier statements. But I had a good feeling while reading this, a sense of wonder I might even say.
