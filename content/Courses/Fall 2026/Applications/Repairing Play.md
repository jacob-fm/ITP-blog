## Introduction

> Yet we embrace this history; it is through resilience that we are united. Despite the pain, we memorialize and share the traumas of the slave trade—and therefore the horrors of colonialism—with one another. The discomfort of living in this place and embodying this history is itself the potential of the Black radical tradition. Embracing and understanding this dissonant place is reparative.

- thinking about this in relation to "infinite players" from [[Finite and Infinite Games]]

> Others believe that the game allowed enslaved Black children a brief moment of liberation by allowing them to roleplay being the “master” (King 2011, 117–8). Both explanations are ultimately **uncomfortable**, as they attempt to reconcile the violence of the experience of Black folk descended from slaves by drawing on the inevitable lighthearted connotations of play.

- the word "uncomfortable" makes me a bit uncomfortable, honestly - I am generally skeptical that discomfort is a good reason to alter our frameworks
	- but it is also possible that Tramell is just being a bit coy and really means that there is something genuinely incorrect or harmful in the explanations he describes here

### Reflections

To be extremely transparent, there is a good chunk of this so far that conjures in me a gut-reaction skepticism, but I am very interested and want to be as open as I can be to receiving the next few chapters. I can easily grant that many forms of play are interchangeable with torture, a notion I think I'm more easily ready to accept after [[Finite and Infinite Games]]. I'm interested to see how ignorance of this idea leads to harm for Black people and other minorities as the author describes. The introduction didn't really win me over on that regard yet, but obviously it's only the introduction.

## Chapter 1: Decolonizing Play

Off the bat, I have to say there is already something dated about tying your argument to the Black Lives Matter movement, whose problems both rhetorically and mechanically have become more clear in the years since.

> The canon of play theory derived from the work of Johan Huizinga (1980) makes a crucial, influential argument that left an indelible mark on a good deal of research that has followed: play itself is productive of civilization. He writes, “Now in myth and ritual the great instinctive forces of civilized life have their origin: law and order, commerce and profit, craft and art, poetry, wisdom and science. All are rooted in the primaeval soil of play” (Huizinga 1980, 5). He implies that “civilized” is all that is legible to Western European “civilization.” The opposite, as I will catalogue here, is barbarism. Likewise, he implies that the civilized is that which is human, while the barbaric is that which is not. Thus, he assumes that people whose customs are not legible to Western Civilization act much like animals. They are like the crows, trifling with baubles with no particular goal or end in mind. For the presumably “civilized,” play is always constructive of something.

- Tramell seems to be quite willing to put a lot of words in Huizinga's mouth, no? Maybe he's referring to things not quoted here, seems unclear.

> We know, just as the crows do, that the project of “civilization” is itself the project of White supremacy.

I can easily agree that the police as an institution are often corrupt, and as Tramell says, are often "guilty of transgressing the very laws that they are sworn to uphold". But it's a big leap from there to "all civilization is White supremacy". I mean surely I'm misunderstanding him, because obviously plenty of civilizations have and currently do exist which are not white at all. Maybe when he says "civilization" here, it's a verb - so the "project of civilization" he refers to here is the project of making people civilized, which certainly has connotations of colonialism, though the quote above is still a bit hard to make sense of, at least for now.

> Norman’s opinion is clearly colored by the unique pressures he faced as the first Black animator hired by Disney. Many “model minorities”— particularly the first BIPOC people in their industry— feel a pressure to perform not just competency at their craft but also cultural competency with the social norms of the community that they enter. Norman defends the inclusion of Black characters in Dumbo, despite the stereotypes they embody, at the expense of critiquing the White supremacist culture from which they had been created. If Norman were to call the crows out as racist, he would be seen as critiquing the company (and thus his peers) as racists. He would violate a social norm and risk being seen as uncivilized. In other words, he would be playing the spoilsport.

This actually bothered me quite a bit. I watched the clip from Dumbo to see for myself. Certainly, the crows depict black stereotypes in the way they talk and move. But as far as depicting stereotypes go, there are a lot more harmful examples you can find. As pointed out in the comments of the YouTube clip I watched, after the crows have a change of heart, they become some of Dumbo's only allies in the film. And really, the most harm they do is tease Dumbo a little bit. This is why I had a bad feeling when we started by appealing to a connection with BLM. The author assumes the reader is so unquestioning that this film is harmful, that the only way to explain the animator's refusal of that fact is to say he's been corrupted by the White supremacist environment to which he is subject. I find it ironic that in our efforts to find the ways minorities have been subjugated, we've denied that this black animator had enough agency to speak his mind a full 78 years after the original film had released (I looked it up).

I'm not saying that it is impossible that Norman, despite being 84 at the time he wrote the blog post (and presumably retired), still felt pressure to conform, or perhaps that he had been Stockholm-syndromed into his beliefs after an entire career working for the man. Only that Tramell's readiness to dismiss Norman's claims in such a way feels rather dismissive or even disrespectful, and that it's starting to seem like in order to appreciate much of Tramell's arguments, the reader needs to come in with a number of prior beliefs in common with Tramell. I'm not sure I have all of those.

I have many more pages to get through, so I hope I will stop writing so much.

> This is why stereotypes of Black people goofing off, having fun, and hanging out are read so negatively—leisure is part of White privilege.

- if the illustrative example of this is the scene from Dumbo, it's a bad one - those characters are positive!

> When they aren’t depicted as animals, the Black men in Dumbo are hard at work. The “jive crows” are contrasted with a Black chain gang (called the Roustabouts) elsewhere in the movie. The Roustabouts are depicted as lazy and drink, smoke, and play instead of work. Particularly offensive are the ways that the Roustabouts sing that their work is “happy,” while swinging heavy hammers. On all levels, the message is clear: If you’re Black you better wear a smile and be “happy” no matter how painful or traumatic your work is. Naturally, at this point I looked up the video and lyrics for "Song of the Roustabouts". I see the author's point - in the song, the Roustabouts do say that even though they're doing backbreaking labor, they're happy when they see that the circus they're building makes children smile, and they don't even care about getting paid. That said, the tone of the video is not joyous and stupid - it's a dreary and perhaps scary scene, where the elephants (who do have faces) are seen to be in bad moods. The song itself is in a minor key and veers on spooky. Having not seen the movie since I was a kid (and not being willing to watch it just for this right now), it's hard for me to say how the scene is meant to be taken.

### Reflections

There was a lot that I didn't like, so here are the things I'm still holding onto, the points that I think are good:

- play encompasses more than just pleasureful play, and we would do good to acknowledge the much greater bounds of play
- European play theorists of the past have theorized about play in a way that reinforces the idea that white European society is the pinnacle of human progress, by reverse engineering their ideas about what makes some play more "advanced" so that patterns of play most common in Europe just-so-happen to be the most "advanced"

## Chapter 3: Play as Capture

> One does not consent to play tag, nor does one offer their consent to become “it” in tag. This, the simplest of games, reveals play is not a relationship between subjects. Instead, it is a relationship between subject and object.

Almost every time I can remember playing tag, it was explicitly consented to, so on instinct I found this ridiculous. But to be more charitable, let's say that where/when Tramell grew up, games of tag were more commonly started by one instigator suddenly tagging someone and saying "you're it", without explicitly asking if their "victim" wanted to play. Even then, as [[Finite and Infinite Games | Carse]] would say, the victim can only play by consenting, ie if they simply do not get up and chase the other players, they are not playing. Of course, there may be social pressure to play regardless of whether the victim *really* wants to, so I can agree that in certain situations, the game of tag can be used as an "othering" tool. Still, when the victim does tag someone else, "it"-ness is transferred immediately, and the subject-object relationship Tramell describes transfers as well. And if it is so easily transferrable, I have a hard time seeing tag as a problem.

> Because consent is central to most scholarly definitions of play, we are left with the paradox explained in the introduction where consensual torture satisfies a definition of play while nonconsensual torture does not. The examples they offer to justify this distinction are almost always formal. They speak more to a desire of what play should be rather than from an observation of what play is. Do we negotiate consent when we play with a computer or with ourselves? Play mediates in ways that are not as straightforward or confined as they may seem at first. In fact, interrogating the mediatory force of play challenges us to reconcile the violence that lies at the heart of innumerable social relationships.

This part is good

> David Leonard argues that in sports video games, in which the presumed White player is invited to take on the role of Black athletes without being forced to live through the trauma of Black experience, play is not negotiated (Leonard 2004, para. 5). The Black community has not consented to this form of identity tourism, yet this sort of minstrelsy is a common form of play.

And what would it mean for the black community to consent to something like that? Who speaks for the black community? And if we're talking about sports games, some quick google searching suggests that at least for some popular sports games, like NBA2K, black fans are likely way over-represented compared to their share of the general U.S. population. (This was not in-depth research, just based on looking up demographics of NBA fandom generally.)

One thing I keep wondering as I read this, is if Tramell will at any point give an example of play that is not problematic. Surely, there has been some good play at some point in human history he can point to?

> In her autoethnographic writing, Emma Vossen explains, “Unfortunately, because of contemporary practices surrounding gameplay, most video gameplay that I have participated in has contained practices that were not consensual or enjoyable, such as harassment, gender-based insults, or trash talk” (Vossen 2018, 206). Appreciating how play is wielded as an instrument of power begins by recognizing accounts of play that would otherwise be lost in a definition that presumes voluntary participation.

I'm interested how to read this in concert with the previous quote above about tag. It seems obvious that in the case of toxic behavior in online games, a player who harasses another does so without consent. But in tag, at least the way I remember it, players consent at the outset to playing the game, though within the bounds of the game they do not consent to being tagged and go out of their way to avoid it. But in that case hardly anyone would say their consent is violated, since at the outset they consented to the game, which can only exist if the possibility of each player becoming "it" is real. It seems like Tramell remembers tag differently than I do, so rather than tag, I'd like to hear his thoughts on something like getting checkmated in chess, where I suppose one could say that the losing player has consented to play the game but in a way has not consented to being checkmated, ie it was done against their will.

> Huizinga’s original theorization of the term argued strongly that voluntarism was a key aspect of play. I believe that this is true for only one of the many parties engaging in play.

So far we've only talked about asymmetric games like tag, but how could this be true for chess, basketball, darts, etc?

> ...it’s important to note here that voluntarism implies that every participant in a game is a player. But what if someone decides they don’t want to play, such as in the example of tag posed earlier? In this example, if one acts as a spoilsport and chooses not to play after they are tagged, they still become “it.” In other words, Huizinga uses terms like spoilsport to allow for play practices where one voluntarily opts out of the play space. What he doesn’t account for are play practices that pull the spoilsport back to the game table even after they have flipped it. After all, a price is paid when one refuses to play.

Glad to see this point addressed. I don't think it's true that a player who refuses to play still becomes "it" - at least that's  not my experience. Regardless, Tramell's point that the "spoilsport" often pays a price for refusing to play is true.

> The killjoy “flips the table” after being subjected to the patriarchal diatribes of her family. She kills the joy of her father, the patriarch, because she aims to resist the discipline of patriarchy. Yet, she is disciplined by him (and the rest of the family) for this act of resistance.

> Are professional athletes working or playing? Is the slog of video game playtesting leisure at all? These questions all play on a binary distinction between labor and leisure. Recognizing that play has encircled and arrested both concepts has great explanatory power here. Understanding play as captivity speaks better to the painful aspects of play in everyday life.

Now we're cooking

> Thus, I argue that brutal, disciplinary torture is always (unfortunately) a form of play...

Why is it unfortunate? That implies it could be some other way than it is, but if it's *always* the case than it can't be. That's like saying "when I don't sleep, I unfortunately feel tired" - which one could say as a joke, but I don't get the sense he's joking here.

> For example, if both participants in tag willingly engage one another in the game, play is then a subject–subject relationship and therefore a consensual relationship.

Phew

### Reflections

There is some core argument here which I find compelling, which goes like this:

1. play is not necessarily pleasurable, e.g. BDSM
2. play is not necessarily consensual, e.g. when one is pressured to play due to external social pressures
	1. (Carse would still consider this consent, but I think it's fair to disagree)
3. ergo, non-consensual, non-pleasurable activities such as torture may be considered play

Tramell sees this as having radical ramifications, the full extent of which I don't quite understand. I get that the dynamics of play often subject BIPOC to abuse. I'm not yet convinced that the BIPOC experience of play is so different from the white experience that the whole book should be oriented around that. I don't understand how, or even if, Tramell's understanding of play is supposed to illuminate something new about police brutality. I don't understand what Tramell is really advocating for in any terms that approach concreteness.
