## Summary

In this essay, John Oswald discusses property, ownership, and copyright as they relate to music making and listening. He points out various ways in which traditional understandings of property rights are applied differently to music than they are to physical property, or even visual media. He makes the point that the lines between music production and reproduction are blurry, and are only getting blurrier as technological means for both production and reproduction become more capable.

## Reflections

These are ideas that I have been thinking about a lot for a few years, so I was very interested in this piece. I hope to make time to reread it. Since high school, I have spent a lot of time making sample-based music, and have spent some time in related online communities, where there is often debate about the legitimacy of sampling. There are plenty of examples of trap and drill beats from the past few years which comprise a sample from a well-known, nostalgia-baiting record from any time between 1980 and the current year, and a trap or drill drum beat laid over it, which itself can be quite formulaic, if the producer even programs it themselves. On the other hand - De La Soul, The Avalanches, etc.

I liked this line from the article:

>Musical language has an extensive repertoire of punctuation devices but nothing equivalent to literature's " " quotation marks. Jazz musicians do not wiggle two fingers of each hand in the air, as lecturers often do, when cross referencing during their extemporizations, because on most instruments this would present some technical difficulties - plummeting trumpets and such.

It made me think of lyrical interpolation, which can also be quite muddy. For example, there's the infamous [clip](https://youtu.be/5Ek0rHF9VxI?si=9roevU5MMQITSamI&t=40) of Soulja Boi on the Breakfast Club accusing Drake of copying his "whole fucking flow", and points out that an early Drake song uses the exact lyrics and cadence from one of his own. Defenders will say this is an homage, and honestly I think they're correct. But it's impossible to know for sure, and I think the general public's takeaway from this clip was that Drake maliciously tried to take credit for someone else's work.

I think Oswald would agree with me when I say these questions are important legally but not artistically. Fortunately or unfortunately, a capitalist society does have to figure out some way of superimposing a legal and artistic lens onto each other in some coherent way if it hopes for art to be a viable career or industry. I enjoyed reading this essay, but regarding how to solve this issue, I don't feel like I have any more of an idea.
