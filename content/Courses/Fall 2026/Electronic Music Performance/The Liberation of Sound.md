I had trouble understanding the first page or so of this text, at least in any intellectual sense. Varese describes music of the future in abstract terms beyond melody. He talks about colliding "sound-masses", "dimensions" of music, and "zones of intensities". There were parts of this discussion that reminded me of feelings I've had at times when listening to music, though I can't say for sure if the moments I'm thinking of are what Varese is describing here.

He then moves on to make some concrete predictions about how music will respond to technological developments, many of which are on the mark. For example, he imagines music that cuts out any interpretation by conductors or players, so that the music he intends is exactly what a listener will hear. He suggests that listeners will be able to hear a piece of music exactly the same every time, in the same way that a book contains exactly the same words every time it is printed. I think we can say confidently that this vision has been realized, as virtual instruments and DAWs have made possible a field of "sound design", where creators can meticulously adjust the particular details, and can programmatically "play" instruments with exact timing and volume. Recording and playback technology have advanced so that the WAV file actually exceeds the printed book in terms of consistency between copies.

Varese ends by warning that we should not expect machines to compose for us, that no matter the means of composing, whether a composition is good or bad will depend on the talent of the composer. Big elephant in the room with this one, but I'm so tired of talking about AI today.

The main thing I'm thinking about is that Varese seemed quite excited by the idea of exact reproductions of recordings, whereas some of my peers and I find ourselves bored or disillusioned with "perfection" in the arts. Perfect copying is actually so trivial, that it is far more interesting when something actually isn't perfect. This is why you find plenty of modern musical recordings that layer the sounds of dust and scratching from record players, or why you can even find effect plugins that simulate the compression effects of mp3 encoding.
