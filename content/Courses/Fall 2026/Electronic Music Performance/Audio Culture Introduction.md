Key points:

In the past several decades, aural media has grown in importance. Due to technological innovations such as the tape recorder, CDs, and eventually digital audio, composers of music and sound have had the means to approach their craft in novel ways. In turn, we have learned to listen to audio differently as well. In particular, the author quotes Pierre Schaeffer's definition of *acousmatic listening*: "listening to sounds in the absence of their original sources and visual contexts, a listening that thus gives access to sound-as-such." These innovations come with an undoing of prior distinctions between genres, and between "high" and "low" art.

Reflections:

I agree with the general sentiment here, though there are particular points that I am as of yet unconvinced of. For example, the author paraphrases Chris Cutler's view that "sound recording has deposed the culture of the eye" (though he does not specifically say he shares this view, he doesn't seem to qualify it). The author later says that "the pristine clarity of digital sound fosters an attention to sonic matter and detail." While it's true that we have better access to accurate recordings than ever before, I am not sure if this is true in a general sense. Do people in 2026 really care more about sonic detail in their recordings than people in prior decades? Maybe.
